\subsection{Motivating Communication Complexity}
\label{ex:motivating-problem}

As data growth transforms the computational landscape, it will become increasingly difficult practically and computationally for individual machines to compute functions. Practically, proprietary company information may create unexpected and consequential gaps in our data. Say we are trying to use air transit to travel from San Diego to Shanghai, however there does not exist a single airline that has both flights out of San Diego and into Shanghai. Using only one airline, we would be unable to find a route. Do we then conclude that no route exists? No, we understand that by taking different airlines for separate legs of our route we can eventually make it to Shanghai. However, do we have to search through every individual flight or is there a technique that we can employ to reduce the amount of flights that we have to search through?

This is the essence of communication complexity. By studying communication complexity, we are trying to find how much information we need to send from each party in order to compute a function.

\subsection{Communication Complexity Basics}

Communication complexity studies the communication requirements to solve problems where inputs are distributed among at least 2 parties with unbounded computing power. The base case of a 2 party problem is typically stated as the following:
\begin{quote}
    There are two players, Alice and Bob who each have a n-bit string, $a$ and $b$ that may or may not be the same. The goal is to compute a function, $f(a, b)$ that depends on both $a$ and $b$.
\end{quote}
Under the two party problem, it is always possible to compute $f(a,b)$ by sending all of $a$ to $b$, effectively reducing problem to a single party. This approach of sending the entire input from one party to another is what we consider the \textit{trivial solution} and is \textit{maximally hard}. We consider this \textit{maximally hard} because it has a communication complexity of $\theta (n)$ bits as all inputs must be sent from $a$ to $b$. One of the most common examples of a \textit{maximally hard} problem is 2 Player Equality:

\begin{claim} 
The deterministic communication complexity of computing the equality between two parties is $\Omega(n)$.
\end{claim}

\begin{proof}
We can represent the universe of two parties with $n$ bit
strings using a $2^n \times 2^n$ boolean matrix $M$. Given two $n$ bit
strings, $a$ and $b$, $M_{i,j} = 1$ if $a_i = b_j$. When proving equality,
if $a=b$ then for all $i = j$, $a_i=b_i$. Therefore, the equality function
can also be represented as the identity matrix. 
It is a well studied fact that the log of the rank of a boolean matrix representing a function is equivalent to the communication complexity of the protocol.
Therefore,
since the equality matrix is the $2^n \times 2^n$ identity matrix, whose
rank is $2^n$, the communication complexity of equality is
$\Omega(\log(2^n)) = \Omega(n)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Graph Traversal Problem}

In this report, we study the problem of traversing a graph which is jointly
owned by many players. Let there be some graph $G=(V,R)$, where $V$ is the set
of all nodes and $E$ is the set of edges. If two or more players ``own'' some
subgraph on the larger graph $G$, how might they identify if they can use their
graphs to collectively construct a path between two arbitrary nodes? Here, we
assume that $G$ is both unweighted and undirected.

More formally, let us denote this problem as:
\begin{quote}
    $TRAV(v,w) = \{0,1\}^n \times ... \times \{0,1\}_k^n \rightarrow \{0,1\}$ where $TRAV(v,w)=1$ if there exists a connected path between $v$ and $w$ such that every edge traversed belongs to a graph in $G_1, G_2,..., G_k$. 
\end{quote}
Each subgraph is a connected subset of the entire graph space G, $G_1, G_2,...,
G_k \subset G$. We consider an edge to belong to a graph if both the starting
and ending vertex of the edge are inside of the subgraph.
